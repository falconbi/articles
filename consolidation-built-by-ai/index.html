Building a Consolidation
Engine with AI

A statutory group consolidation in TM1, built by an AI through an MCP, test-first against 26 published examples. Four days. This is how it went: the phases, the research, the test data, the design, the mistakes.

01

The setup

A blank TM1 v11 server. A browser IDE for TM1. An MCP that lets the AI build and inspect the model: dimensions, cubes, rules, processes, views, and stored checks.

I gave it the goal, my old consolidation design notes as background, and my rules of practice. I approved each major step.

02

The method: test first

Nothing was built until there was a worked example with a known answer to build against.

Every check is a stored query with an expected number. All of them run every time the model changes. A change that breaks an earlier example does not get through. By the end: 26 examples, 133 checks.

03

Research: the standards

The AI worked from the IFRS standards as issued, not local variants. The ones that shaped the build:

StandardWhat it decided
IFRS 10consolidate on control, not ownership %; buying or selling NCI is an equity transaction; loss of control
IFRS 3goodwill at the date control is gained; earlier stakes revalued; negative goodwill is a gain; one goodwill per business combination
IAS 21closing, average and historic rates; translation reserve; goodwill of a foreign subsidiary retranslated; reserve recycled on disposal
IFRIC 16direct vs step-by-step translation of a sub-group
IAS 28associates: equity method

Where my old textbook used pre-2008 rules — goodwill per tranche, goodwill written off to reserves — the AI flagged it and adapted the examples to current IFRS rather than copy the old journals.

04

Research: finding test data

The AI searched for worked consolidation examples with published answers. Exam questions were the best source: full facts, full answer.

SourceUsed for
ACCA Student Accountant articlesvertical groups, mixed (D-shaped) groups, foreign disposal
ACCA P2 / SBR exam answersstep acquisition, NCI trades, loss of control, a full master group, a foreign subsidiary
ACCA FR specimenassociates
Ng Eng Juan, Consolidated Financial Statementsseven examples: consolidation of consolidation, connecting affiliates, intragroup P&L

Some things had no published example: a year-end roll-forward across two years, three currencies in one group. For those the AI built the case and wrote out the workings by hand for me to check. The difference is marked on every check.

06

How the design came about

The AI started from my first design — one dimension of ownership paths. It proposed a different shape: separate dimensions for whose books, which company, the other side, and which adjustment.

Rather than argue it, we built both, in the same model, on the same data, and ran both against every example.

Both reached the book's answers. The first needed three corrections to get there. The second needed none and has less to maintain. The evidence made the decision.

07

What the AI found

  • A textbook answer that did not balance. One of the seven book examples was out by 42. The AI reworked it from the facts; both designs matched the correction.
  • A gap in my first design. When a sub-group has its own goodwill, the outside shareholders of the sub-group took a share of it. Goodwill and NCI were 4.4 too high. The fix is the indirect holding adjustment.
  • A bug in its own engine, found by comparing the two designs: one business combination was being split into goodwill on one company and a gain on another.
  • A silent failure mode. With TM1's SKIPCHECK on and feeders missing, a subsidiary disappeared with no error. It built a check that runs before every consolidation.
  • Bugs in the IDE itself. A rules linter that miscounted arguments, a cube map that could not see processes. Fixed along the way.
08

What the AI got wrong

  • Built cubes without default views and dimensions without default subsets. I found it.
  • Let the main process grow to about 1,200 lines in one block. I found it. Splitting it is on the list.
  • Started actions — even read-only checks — before I had said yes. I stopped it. The rule now: nothing runs without explicit approval.
  • Lost the model's data once, after the server stopped uncleanly before a save. It rebuilt everything from its saved test files in minutes. A save now runs hourly.
  • Gave long answers when I needed one step at a time. Asked, it changed.
09

What the human did

The AI did the building and the arithmetic. The decisions that mattered came from experience:

  • Classify by control, measure NCI by ownership.
  • Store movements, not balances.
  • Journals run once per close, cleared and rebuilt — not live rules.
  • SKIPCHECK and feeders, always.
  • Every cube a default view, every dimension a default subset.
  • Keep the worked examples in the model, permanently.
  • Choose between the designs on evidence.

Then I checked it — one example at a time, journal by journal, against the book.

10

Lessons

Test data first. Published examples with answers turned "looks right" into "is right". Without them I would not trust any of it.

  • The AI is fast and thorough at research, arithmetic and building. It still needs a domain expert to set the rules and to check.
  • Build both options when unsure. It is cheaper than arguing.
  • Every change in a change set, every result against a check. That is what makes speed safe.
  • House rules must be written down. The AI follows what it is told, not what it should have guessed.
  • Keep the test files outside the server. They were the recovery.

Four days for a tested statutory consolidation engine. The design is in the companion article; the detail of each step is in its detailed section.
